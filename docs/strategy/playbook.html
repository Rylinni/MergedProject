Onion & Furnace Playbook

How the trained agents play the 5×5 merge game, written as rules you can follow at the table. Part 1 is for the highest total score. Part 2 is a separate plan for the single biggest merge.

Vocabulary

Names for the patterns

Onion
Values rising from the outside in: 1–3 on the rim (1s in corners), 4–5 on rim and ring, 6s on the ring diagonals, Ms on the hub and plus cells.
Furnace
The hub and its four plus cells, where Ms are parked as a pair waiting for the third M.
Loaded pair
Two touching equal tiles with at least one empty neighbour, so one matching tile merges them.
Dead pair
A pair with no empty neighbour. It cannot be merged until something nearby clears.
Buried tile
A tile with no empty neighbour at all.
Ladder (height k)
An empty cell where one tile would chain k times, e.g. a 2 pair and a 3 pair both touching it is a height-2 ladder.
Hand-off
A domino chain in which the small half climbs to the big half's value, then the big half merges through it and the chain continues from the big half's cell.
Socket: S and F
Two empty touching cells waiting for a domino. S (starter) takes the small half; F (fire cell) takes the big half and is where the explosion happens.
Rung
One step of a ladder: a pair (or single) of the right value touching S or F.
Out
A piece that would fire a setup at full height.
Cash-out
Firing part of a ladder early (a 1–3 step merge) to win back space. It gives up the big setup.
Debris
Tiles destroyed by an explosion that were not in any merging group. In a 6-step chain each debris point is worth ×720.
Dump
Corners and edge cells where new 1s and 2s go.
Part 1 · Playing for the highest score

Eight rules, most important first

From 300 games by the score model, 300 by a greedy player (takes the best immediate merge, likes empty cells) and the 179,706-point record game, all replayed move by move.

1

Space comes first

Keep about 14 cells empty. Below 10, take every merge you are offered.

Games end fast once space starts to go: over the final 20 moves the board loses about one empty cell per move. The AI keeps a cushion and turns down merges only when it can afford to.

Every game hit 5 or fewer empty cells at some point. After that the AI lasted a median 953 more moves; the greedy player lasted 19. With 4 or fewer empty cells it declined 6 merges in 3,113 moves.

2

Run a furnace in the centre

Park Ms as a pair on the hub and a plus cell. Fire every third M as soon as it arrives.

After M unlocks, one piece value in seven is an M, and an M only leaves the board by exploding. A centre blast clears the full 3×3; on the rim it clears as few as 4 cells.

The AI keeps 44% of its Ms on the hub and 3.7% on the rim (greedy: 56% on the rim). When an explosion was possible it fired 99% of the time. 94% of explosions happened on the hub or ring, clearing 3.5 tiles besides the Ms.

3

Build the onion

Put new 1s and 2s in corners and on edges. Keep the middle for high tiles.

Merged results land where you place, so placing low and outside pushes growth inward toward the furnace, where it can be blasted.

Low tiles that don't merge go to a corner 33%, an edge 61%, the ring 6%. The greedy player's layout is flat, with 37% of its low tiles landing in the middle.

4

When there's room, make a pair instead of a small merge

With plenty of space, pass on merges worth a dozen points and place the tile to make a loaded pair.

A pair is stored value: later it can be one rung of a chain worth ×2 to ×24.

The AI declined 18.9% of available merges (greedy 1.5%); 67.5% of those declines made a new pair. The median merge it gave up was worth 12 points. It almost never declines an explosion (0.7%).

5

Keep every pair loaded

Don't seal a pair in with unrelated tiles. Every tile should have a way to be reached.

98% of the AI's pairs have an empty neighbour (greedy 86%). It averages 1.1 buried tiles per board; greedy averages 2.3.

6

Stack pairs into ladders

Place pairs of consecutive values (2-2 beside 3-3, 3-3 beside 4-4) so both touch one empty cell.

37% of the AI's boards hold a ladder of height 2 or more (greedy 18.5%). Its 4-step chains are 4× as common as greedy's (0.73% vs 0.19% of moves).

7

Dominoes: feed two groups, or hand off

weak evidence

Look for a placement where each half completes its own pair, or where the small half climbs into the big one.

About half of dominoes merge. Of those, 8.1% hand off and 7.3% merge both halves separately, but greedy hands off almost as often (5.9%), so this may not be a learned skill.

8

Down to about 4 empty cells: cash out and bomb

Take every merge, and use an M explosion to clear the most crowded part of the board.

With 4 or fewer empty cells the AI merges on 49% of moves. Boards at game over had about 7 different values and 19 separate groups: the end comes from too many values competing for space, not from one unlucky piece.

Part 2 · Playing for the largest single merge

One huge move: the ×720 socket

This is a different game. The big-merge model averages about 5,700 points per game instead of 25,000, and it holds dangerous boards on purpose. What it buys is the 6-step chain, the longest chain the rules allow.

The target shape

Each step of a chain needs a fresh group touching the cell that's merging, and a cell has only 4 neighbours. A single piece therefore stops at 4 steps. A domino gets 6, because the chain hands off from S to F and F brings 3 new neighbours. All 90 six-step chains the AI played were one of two dominoes, and every one ended in an explosion next to the hub:

2+4 (74 of 90) 2→3→4 hand-off to F 5→6→M→boom
2+5 (16 of 90) 2→3→4→5 hand-off to F 6→M→boom

Points = (sum of every tile merged or blown up) × 720. Groups of exactly three give 3 × (2+3+4+5+6+7) = 81, so 58,320 is the standard result. Extra tiles in a group or in the blast raise it: the AI's best was 65,520 (91 × 720). The best board our search found scores 70,560 (98 × 720). A domino that contains an M can't do this: its other half reaches M in at most 3 steps, so the chain caps at ×24.

A real socket one move before it fired for 58,320 (example E below). S and F are the dashed cells; the waiting piece is a 2+4 domino.

Building the socket (2+4 version)

  1. Pick F on a plus cell, next to the hub. All 90 six-step explosions fired from a plus cell. Your M pair (the furnace) should already touch it.
  2. Around F, on its three other neighbours: the M pair, a 6 pair and a 5 pair. Only one tile of each pair has to touch F; the partner can lie further out.
  3. Pick S as F's neighbour on the rim side. Around S: a 2 pair, a 3 pair, and one single 4 touching S or F. That lone 4 joins the domino's 4 and the climbing 4 at the hand-off. For 2+5 instead: pairs of 2, 3 and 4 around S, and a single 5 touching F.
  4. Keep S and F empty, and leave no other cell where a stray 2 or 3 would set off part of the ladder.
  5. Wait for the out. A particular domino comes about 2.4% of the time (roughly one in 40 pieces). A 3+4 also fires a 2+4 socket, at 5 steps, for about 9,000–10,000.

Build low rungs first, the M pair last

How long each rung had been in place when the 6-step fired (median moves, 91 chains). The 3 and 2 pairs on the rim go in first; the M pair is completed last most often (28 of 91).

Protect the M pair: with a 6-step ready, the big-merge model refused to explode a new M 71 times out of 78, parking it elsewhere. The score model explodes 99% of the time.

When to hold and when to bail out

Hold while you have 5 or more empty cells. Take a 5-step out (3+4) if it comes first. At about 3 empty cells, cash out.

Of 300 times a 6-step was ready (2-step search), 9% fired the full six, 7% settled for five, and most were lost. 223 of 251 losses happened at 2–4 empty cells, usually by cashing out part of the ladder. Ready boards averaged 5.5 empty cells.

Load the blast

At ×720, every point of debris matters: one M caught in the blast adds 7 × 720 = 5,040. Debris comes from F's diagonal neighbours. Leave 5s, 6s or a spare M there, as long as they don't touch a rung of the same value. The AI barely does this (54 of 90 chains had no debris), so this tip comes from the arithmetic, not from watching the AI.

Phases

The game in three parts

Opening · up to about move 9

Pairs and corners

Only 1s, 2s and 3s exist. Dump 1s in corners and start pairs. About a third of moves merge. Short phase, thin evidence.

Middle · 4 to 6 unlocked, about 18 moves

Grow inward

The busiest merging phase (46% of moves). Steer growing tiles inward so the first 6s land on the ring diagonals and the first M near the hub. 6 unlocks around move 20, M around move 26.

Late · M unlocked, over 98% of all moves

The real game

Furnace, onion and space discipline (rules 1–3), pairs over small merges when there's room. For a big merge, this is when you build the socket: the 6-step needs an M pair.

Limits

What this guide can't tell you

  • These are patterns in what the AI does, not proven causes. No rule was tested by switching it off.
  • The greedy comparison player is weak, so "better than greedy" doesn't show that each habit matters on its own.
  • Rule 7 (domino technique) shows little difference from greedy play.
  • The spawn odds (every unlocked value equally likely, half the pieces dominoes) are assumed, not confirmed for the iOS game. The opening is very short in this simulator, so the opening advice is thin.
  • The debris tip is arithmetic. The 70,560 board was found by search; no agent has played it.
  • Big-merge statistics rest on 90 six-step chains and 300 ready-to-fire episodes.
Data: 300 games by the score model (606,278 moves), 300 greedy games, 3,200 big-merge games, the 179,706-point record game and the 65,520-move game. All games use the rules confirmed from screen recordings of the iOS game.
